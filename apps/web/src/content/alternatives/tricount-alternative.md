Tricount is good at the part it is built for. It says so plainly, and it is right:

<Quote source="tricount.com">
tricount helps you keep track of who paid what. Invite your friends, add your expenses, focus on the fun.
</Quote>

If that is the problem you have, you do not need an alternative. The reason people go looking for one is almost always the same, and it is not the maths — it is what happens after the maths.

## Look at how it expects you to settle up

Two lines from Tricount's own homepage describe the settle path, and both assume something about your group.

<Quote source="tricount.com">
Send a payment request straight from the app and get paid directly to your bank account.
</Quote>

<Quote source="tricount.com">
Pay with your bunq card and the expense gets automatically added to your tricount.
</Quote>

Both are genuinely useful — if the group shares the rails. A payment request that lands in a bank account works when the person paying it can reach that account cheaply. The card integration works when you hold that card. Tricount is owned by bunq, a European bank, and the smoothest version of the product is the one where your group banks the way it expects.

A group of four in Berlin will have a fine time. A group of four in Berlin, Buenos Aires, Lisbon and Bangkok will get to a correct number and then spend three weeks not paying it.

## What Split does differently

| | Peanut Split | Tricount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Getting in | Open a link, type a name | "Download for free" from the App Store or Google Play |
| Cost | Free, no paid tier | "100% free" |
| Doing the maths | Balances update live, reconciles to the cent | "tricount does the math for you" |
| Settling up | Whatever the two people can actually use — cash, a local transfer, or a payment link | "get paid directly to your bank account", or a bunq card |
| Best case | Group spread across countries, banks and currencies | Group that shares banking rails |

The row that matters is the fourth one. Everything above it is table stakes that both products clear.

## The three things that break a cross-border group

<Checklist title="Where a split actually fails">
<ChecklistItem title="Nobody has the same app">"Just send it to me" collapses when one person has Revolut, one has a bank that shuts at four, and one is in a country neither reaches. The fix is not a better app — it is not requiring a particular one.</ChecklistItem>
<ChecklistItem title="You paid in one currency and owe in another">Pick what the room counts in, add expenses in whatever you actually spent, and Split converts. Free, and not a feature behind a tier.</ChecklistItem>
<ChecklistItem title="Two people never joined">Every install you ask for is a person who drops out. A Split room is a link, so the person who was never going to make an account still adds their taxi fare.</ChecklistItem>
</Checklist>

## What we are not going to claim

Split is smaller than Tricount and does less.

There is no account, which means no login, no password recovery, and no history that follows you to a new phone. The link is the key: if your group loses it, the room is gone. There is no card that logs your spending for you, and there is no bank behind us doing the transfer — when two people settle, they settle, and Split records that it happened.

That is the trade. Less product, fewer assumptions about who you bank with.

<Callout title="Free forever, and what that means">
No fees, no premium tier, nothing to upgrade to. Split exists because Peanut wants people moving money across borders, not because it sells subscriptions. If that ever changes, this page changes before anything else does.
</Callout>
