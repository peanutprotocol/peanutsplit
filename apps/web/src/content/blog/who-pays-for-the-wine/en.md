# Who pays for the wine

Somebody at the table did not drink. They had the tap water, they had one course, and they have spent the last hour listening to a discussion of a second bottle they did not order. The bill arrives face down. Someone turns it over, looks at the total, and says the line that closes the subject before it can open. "Shall we do it evenly."

An even split gets treated as the absence of a decision, which is why it is so hard to argue with. It is a decision. It moves the cost of the group's most expensive appetites onto everybody at the table, and what it buys in return is a table where nobody has to price their own evening out loud. Most of the time that is a good trade. Sometimes it is not, and the reason those dinners end quietly is that nobody present has the language to say which of the two they are in.

Four conventions are in general use. Naming them is most of the work, because an argument about a bill is almost never an argument about arithmetic. It is two people running different conventions, and neither of them saying which.

## The even split

What it buys is speed, and something better than speed: nobody has to declare what they had. The person who ordered the cheapest thing on the menu is not made to announce it, and the person who ordered the second bottle is not made to defend it. In a group that eats together often, this costs nothing at all, because the subsidy rotates. Tonight you drink and somebody else drives.

It stops working the moment the difference stops rotating. A table where two people are three rounds in and two people are on tap water is not a rotation, it is a transfer, and it is a transfer the person on the losing side can see, because they are reading the same bill. The convention's entire defence is that the gap is too small to be worth naming. When the gap is a third of the total, the defence is gone and the convention carries on regardless.

## The drinkers' carve-out

Take the alcohol off the top, divide it between the people who drank it, split the food evenly. That is most of the asymmetry gone, because drink is where the spread almost always lives: it is ordered late, ordered by the round, priced by the bottle, and printed as its own block of lines. Nothing else on a restaurant bill varies that much between two people who ate the same meal.

The cost is not arithmetic, it is sequence. Somebody has to say it out loud, and whoever says it becomes, in that moment, the person who is counting. The non-drinker is the worst placed person to raise it, which is precisely why they are usually the one left to. The convention works when a drinker proposes it. From the person who ordered the wine, "take the bottles off and we'll cover those" is generosity. From the person on tap water, the same instruction is a complaint. Identical split, identical numbers, completely different dinner.

## Itemising

Assign every line to whoever ordered it. What this buys is a number nobody can argue with, which in a group of near-strangers is worth more than the money.

What it costs is that an evening becomes a reconciliation, and that everyone's choices get priced in front of each other. It also scales badly downwards. Recovering four euros from a friend costs more than four euros, every time. Itemising earns its place when the bill is long, the group is large, and the people in it are not close: a stag weekend, a fortnight in a villa, eleven people of whom four are somebody's colleagues. There the accuracy is the courtesy. Nobody subsidises a stranger and nobody has to trust an average.

## The quiet subsidy

The fourth convention is the most common and the least discussed, because it is never proposed. It is defaulted into. One person notices the asymmetry, works out that raising it would cost more than absorbing it, and absorbs it. Nothing is said. The dinner ends well.

It ends well once. Starling Bank's survey of two thousand UK adults found that 46% of people owed money by friends or family were too embarrassed to ask for it, that getting it back took more than two months on average, and that 17% had begun to resent the person who owed them. The last of those is the one that matters here, because resentment is the price of a subsidy nobody was told they were receiving. The person absorbing it is keeping a ledger. The person benefiting does not know a ledger exists. CNBC's reporting on the etiquette of asking names the mechanism exactly: the memory of being owed is elephant-like, and the memory of owing is not.

A subsidy given knowingly, once, is a gift. A subsidy that runs for a year is a grievance with a start date.

## Three harder cases

The vegetarian who had the €9 salad and pays a share of somebody's €34 steak is the standard example, and the weakest of them, because on any single night the difference is small. What makes it hurt is repetition. The useful response is not to itemise this dinner. It is to notice, around the tenth, that the same person has been on the subsidising side of all ten, and to change the convention rather than the maths.

Bottles ordered "for the table" are the genuinely hard case, because shared is a claim about intent rather than about consumption. Two bottles arrive for eight people and four of them drink both. Nobody lied. The order really was placed on behalf of the table, consumption diverged from intent afterwards, and no line on the bill records that it did. Only a convention rescues this one: either the table treats shared bottles as shared however they get finished, or it treats drink as per-person, and it decides which before the second bottle.

Arriving at nine to a table that sat down at seven is the clean case, and still the one groups get wrong most often. The pool was agreed before you got there, and the agreement is doing the work. The fix is timing rather than fairness, and the timing is on arrival rather than over the bill.

## Is everyone going to split the loo roll

This is the objection every fairness argument runs into, and it deserves an answer rather than a dismissal. It turns up in close to these words wherever people argue about splitting a household:

<Quote source="forums.moneysavingexpert.com">
So is everyone going to also split how many times they use the loo/ put the lights on / spend time in the kitchen?
</Quote>

Fairness has a granularity floor, and everything worth doing happens above it. Three tests put an asymmetry above the line. It is large. It is visible on the bill. It is attributable to named people without anyone having to reconstruct the evening from memory. Wine clears all three. The loo roll clears none, and measuring it would cost more in argument than the whole sum in dispute, which is the real case against it, not squeamishness about fairness.

Naming the two or three asymmetries above the floor is not the first step towards itemising a life. It is what saves you from having to. Groups that end up counting everything are usually groups that declined to name the one large thing, and then had to relitigate every bill after it.

## What to say, and when

Three sentences do nearly all of this work, and none of them name anybody. Before the menus are collected, from whoever is nearest the middle of the table: "Are we splitting evenly, or doing drinks separately?" From a drinker, once the second bottle is on its way: "Put the wine on us at the end." From somebody arriving late, in the first minute rather than the last: "I will get mine separately."

All three are logistics when they are said early. All three are accusations when they are said over the bill. The words do not change. The clock does.

## The honest bit

None of this is enforceable, and none of it survives being introduced late. A method produced after the total is known reads as a position rather than a policy, however sound the arithmetic behind it. If nothing was agreed before ordering, the cheapest option left is to split evenly and set the convention next time.

There is also a floor below which the exercise costs more than it recovers. If the whole disputed amount across a month of dinners comes to less than one of those dinners, the conversation is the expensive part.

And no tool does the social half. Split states the figure and shows how it got there, which is the half people argue about least. It holds no opinion about which convention your table should use, it does not check with a bank and cannot, and settling up is a tap that records what two people already did.

What is worth taking from a bill you resented is not a formula. It is that the convention was never agreed, and that agreeing one takes about eleven seconds while the menus are still on the table. If it helps to have the running total held somewhere other than in your own head, a Split room is a link, and it will keep count without joining the argument.
