Here is a dinner in Bangkok, paid by one person, split four ways.

The bill was 3,200 baht. The person who paid has a card that bills in pounds, so their statement eventually says £71.40. The group agreed the trip counts in euros. Three of the four owe money on it.

There are now at least three defensible numbers for the same dinner, and if different people use different ones the group's totals will not add up. Not by much — a percent here, two percent there — but enough that someone notices, and then nobody trusts the ledger.

## The rule: one rate, agreed once

The specific rate matters much less than people expect. What matters is that every expense in the room goes through the same one.

Mixing rates is what breaks things. If Tuesday's dinner is converted at Tuesday's rate, Thursday's taxi at Thursday's, and the villa at whatever the booking site charged, then the sum of everyone's shares no longer equals the sum of the expenses. The gap has to land somewhere, and it lands on whoever did the arithmetic last.

<Callout title="What to agree on, in one sentence">
"Everything converts at the rate the room used when we started." That is it. It is not the most accurate possible answer for any single expense, and it is the only answer that keeps the totals honest.
</Callout>

## Enter the amount you actually paid

Put in 3,200 baht. Not €84, not £71.40, not your phone's guess from the taxi queue.

Two reasons. The receipt says 3,200 — it is the one number in the whole chain that is not an estimate, and if the group ever needs to check an expense, that is what they will check against. And your own card's number is not the group's business: your bank's spread is a cost of your card, not a cost of the dinner. Push it into the shared total and you have quietly asked three friends to subsidise your choice of bank.

So: log the local amount, let the room convert once, and keep your statement out of it.

## Your card's rate is a separate problem

Worth saying plainly, because this is where people feel cheated and blame the wrong thing.

When you pay 3,200 baht on a card that bills in pounds, your bank picks a rate and usually adds something to it. That happens between you and your bank, days later, and no expense splitter can see it or change it. If your statement says £71.40 and the group's maths says the equivalent of £69.80, the £1.60 is your card's margin.

That is not the split being wrong. It is the cost of having paid on that card, and the only fix for it is a different card — or being the person who pays in cash.

<Checklist title="Keeping a multi-currency room clean">
<ChecklistItem title="Pick the room currency before the first expense">Choose whatever most of the spending is actually in, not whatever your own bank uses. Changing it halfway is what forces a re-conversion of everything already logged.</ChecklistItem>
<ChecklistItem title="Log local amounts, always">The currency on the receipt is the currency in the room. Converting at entry throws away the provable number.</ChecklistItem>
<ChecklistItem title="Settle in the room's currency">If the room counts in euros, the transfers at the end are in euros. Converting again on the way out reintroduces exactly the gap you just avoided.</ChecklistItem>
<ChecklistItem title="Expect cents, not percentages">A clean room reconciles to the cent. If it is out by a few percent, somebody used a different rate somewhere.</ChecklistItem>
</Checklist>
