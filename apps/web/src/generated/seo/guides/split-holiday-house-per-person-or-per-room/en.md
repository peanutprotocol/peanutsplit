Nine of you take a cottage in the Yorkshire Dales for a week, and somebody asks whether the
six-year-old pays before anybody has packed a bag; get that wrong quietly and one family pays for
a bedroom nobody used, says nothing about it, and finds a reason not to come next year. Let Split
hold the week's expenses. It keeps the cottage and the shopping in one shared Split room,
so the numbers sit where the whole group can read them.

Every method below charges either by the bedroom or by the head, then bends for a child, a late
arrival, and a case of wine four of you drink.

## Holiday cottage: pay per person or per room

Per room charges for what you were given: a bed, a door, a bathroom you do not queue for. Per person
charges for the week itself and gives everybody the same claim on the house. Booking.com's guide to
splitting a rental names the snag in the per-person version.

> This method has drawbacks when children are involved, as they often qualify for discounted rates
> and may not contribute equally to the rental cost.

*Booking.com, "How to split holiday rental costs: a group holiday guide", checked August 2026*

Per room has its own version of that problem.

The cottage is £2,080 for seven nights: an ensuite double, two standard doubles and a bunk room.
Nine people go, in three households, and six of them are adults. The children are fourteen, ten and
six.

### Per room, with a premium on the best bedroom

A flat quarter each is £520 a bedroom, which nobody who has seen the ensuite will agree to. Weight
the bedrooms instead. Count a standard double as one unit, the ensuite as 1.25 and the bunk room as
0.75: four units in all, and £2,080 over four units is £520 a unit.

| Bedroom | Weight | Cost | Who sleeps there |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ensuite double | 1.25 | £650 | the two adults of the family of four |
| Double | 1 | £520 | the two adults of the family of three |
| Double | 1 | £520 | the couple |
| Bunk room | 0.75 | £390 | the three children |
| Total | 4 | £2,080 | |

The bunk room is the awkward one. Three children from two households sleep in it, so it comes apart
by heads: £260 to the family with two in there, £130 to the family with one. Household totals land
at £910 for the family of four, £650 for the family of three and £520 for the couple.

### Per person, with a weight for the children

Weight the people instead. Adults count as one, under-twelves a half, and the fourteen-year-old
counts as one because the line sits at twelve. That is seven full shares and two halves, so £2,080
over eight shares is £260 a share.

| Household | Shares | Working | Pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family of four | 3 | two adults plus two half shares | £780 |
| Family of three | 3 | two adults plus a fourteen-year-old | £780 |
| The couple | 2 | two adults | £520 |
| Total | 8 | | £2,080 |

<Callout type="info">
**Both methods collect £2,080.** The couple pays £520 either way, and the whole decision moves £130
from one family to the other.
</Callout>

## How to split family holiday costs with children

The half share is a convention, not a fact about children. The total does not fall because a guest
is six, so the group is choosing to charge one family less. Say that out loud when you draw the
line. Twelve is where the example above draws it; pick your own, write it next to the total, and the
numbers follow it.

### Whether a child counts as a full share

The answer changes line by line. On the house a small child takes half a share, because the roof is
what the money buys. On the food the same child is a whole head. On the wine they count for nothing.
A weight belongs to the expense, not to the person.

## When a household stays fewer nights

The couple arrives on the Monday and gets five of the seven nights. Multiply each household's
shares by the nights it was there: 21, 21 and 10 makes 52 share-nights, and £2,080 over 52 is £40 a
share-night.

| Household | Shares | Nights | Share-nights | Pays |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family of four | 3 | 7 | 21 | £840 |
| Family of three | 3 | 7 | 21 | £840 |
| The couple | 2 | 5 | 10 | £400 |
| Total | | | 52 | £2,080 |

The house cost £2,080 whether anybody slept in it on the Sunday, so part-nights make nothing
cheaper. Against the per-person shares of £780, £780 and £520, they move £120 off the couple and
onto the two families, £60 each. Whether that is right is for the three households to agree between
them. [A household that pulls out altogether](/guides/someone-drops-out-of-a-group-trip) is a
different question with three defensible answers.

## Groceries by heads, wine among the drinkers

Food is where per person wins outright, because food is bought by the mouth, and children are whole
heads here. Drink comes off that bill and goes to the people drinking it.

| Line | Total | Split across | Each |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shopping | £405 | 9 heads | £45 |
| Wine | £96 | 4 drinkers | £24 |

The alternative is a running tally on one person's phone, corrected in the group chat every time
somebody buys milk: a week of accounts kept in a kitchen you do not live in, for a household that
dissolves on the Saturday. Bin the tally!

## Keep the method and the expenses in one place

Paste the room link into the group chat with the agreed method written under it. The link is the
key, so whoever holds it can open the room and add to it. There is no login and no password
recovery, so it needs to stay somewhere the whole group can still find it in a month.

By Friday the cottage is on one card, the shopping on another and the wine on a third. Split nets
those debts down, and the week ends on a short payment plan. Only the names on the transfers
change, never the household totals, and
[why you owe someone you never paid](/guides/why-do-i-owe-someone-i-never-paid) proves it with
three people and two receipts.

### What to send with the number

- Say which method it came from, and where the age line was drawn.
- Keep the house, the food and the drink on separate lines.
- Put each household's total next to the people it covers.

## Recording the payments once they happen

Settle however you like: cash, a bank transfer, or another payment app. Then somebody records it in the room. Split
records the payment, it does not make it. It does not verify with any bank and cannot. The room
holds the shares and collects nothing from anybody.


## When a shared note is the better tool

Two nights, two households and a total you could settle at the door: divide it by the people, write
the one number in the shared note, and leave the weights out of it.
[Asking for that one number back](/guides/ask-a-friend-to-pay-you-back) is a message, not a room. On
a short stay, running both methods to compare them costs more attention than the gap between them is
worth. A Split room earns
its place when several people paid for different things and the settling happens after everyone has
driven home. It does not make anybody pay and it does not decide what a child is worth; it keeps
the working where both families can check it.
